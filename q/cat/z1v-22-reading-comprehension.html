Passage: Passage: Notes by hand and notes by keyboard
Passage: Notes by hand and notes by keyboard

Students who carry laptops into lectures can type almost as fast as a teacher speaks. Students who write by hand cannot; even a quick writer manages perhaps a third of what is said. At first sight this looks like a clear win for the laptop: more words captured should mean more material to revise from. Yet several classroom studies have suggested that students who take notes by hand often do as well as, or better than, typists on later questions that test understanding.

One explanation lies in the very slowness of handwriting. A student writing by hand cannot copy every sentence, so she has to decide what matters and put it in her own words. That act of choosing and rephrasing forces her to think about the ideas while she hears them. A fast typist, by contrast, can take down the lecture almost word for word without stopping to process it. The notes are longer, but less of the thinking has been done.

This does not mean laptops are useless. Typed notes are easier to search, share and reorganise, and for students with certain disabilities a keyboard may be essential. Nor do the studies show that handwriting is magic. Their point is narrower: what helps learning is the effort of summarising, not the pen itself. The same studies found that when typists were simply told not to copy word for word, many of them carried on doing so, because the habit of transcribing is hard to break when the keyboard makes it so easy. A typist who deliberately writes short summaries in her own words, rather than transcribing, may gain much of the same benefit.

Laptops bring another risk that has nothing to do with typing speed. A screen opens the way to messages, shopping and videos, and a student who drifts to them misses parts of the lecture. Some studies have found that this also distracts the students sitting nearby, who can see the screen. Teachers who ban laptops usually mention this problem first, ahead of any argument about memory, because it is the one they can see from the front of the room every day.

The practical lesson is modest, and it applies well beyond the lecture hall. Whether they use a pen or a keyboard, students learn more when they treat note-taking as a way of thinking rather than as a way of recording. The tool matters less than the habit.

According to the passage, why might handwriting help understanding?
AHandwritten notes are easier to search later.
BWriting slowly forces students to choose and rephrase the important ideas.
CPens are less distracting than laptops for nearby students.
DTeachers speak more slowly when students write by hand.
Show answer & solution

Correct answer: B. Writing slowly forces students to choose and rephrase the important ideas.

Solution: Paragraph 2: because a hand-writer cannot copy everything, 'she has to decide what matters and put it in her own words', which makes her think about the ideas. Option A is a benefit of typed notes.
