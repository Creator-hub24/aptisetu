Passage: How a beehive keeps its temperature

A honeybee colony behaves in some ways like a single warm-blooded animal. The developing young bees, or brood, need a steady temperature of about 34 to 35 degrees Celsius. A few degrees too cold and they develop slowly or with defects; a few degrees too hot and they may die. The adult workers therefore spend a great deal of their energy keeping the brood area at just the right warmth, whatever the weather outside. The rest of the hive, where honey and pollen are stored, is allowed to vary much more. The colony, in other words, does not heat or cool its whole home evenly; it protects the part that matters most for its future.

When the air is cold, workers cluster tightly over the brood. Many of them produce heat by rapidly tensing the large flight muscles in their bodies without moving their wings, much as a person shivers. A bee doing this can raise its own body temperature well above that of the air around it. Bees on the outside of the cluster pack closely together and act like an insulating blanket, while those inside generate the warmth. The work is tiring, and bees on the cold outer layer gradually move inwards to warm up while others take their place.

Heat is the opposite problem in summer. Workers line up at the entrance and fan their wings to drive air through the hive. On very hot days others fly out to collect water, spread thin films of it over the comb and fan it so that it evaporates. Evaporation takes heat away, so the hive is cooled in roughly the way a damp cloth cools a clay pot.

No single bee is in charge of any of this. Each worker reacts to the temperature it senses nearby, and individuals seem to differ slightly in the temperature at which they start to fan or to shiver. Because of these small differences, the colony does not swing wildly between all-out heating and all-out cooling. As conditions change, a few bees respond first, then more join in, and the response grows smoothly.

Beekeepers have learned to respect this system. Opening a hive on a cold morning, for instance, can undo hours of careful warming, and a hive placed in full afternoon sun forces the colony to spend its effort fetching water instead of food. Good management, in short, means making the bees' own work easier rather than replacing it.

According to the passage, how do bees produce heat in cold weather?
ABy flying in circles inside the hive.
BBy tensing their flight muscles rapidly without moving their wings.
CBy eating more honey than usual.
DBy sealing the hive entrance with wax.
Show answer & solution

Correct answer: B. By tensing their flight muscles rapidly without moving their wings.

Solution: Paragraph 2 says it directly: they produce heat 'by rapidly tensing the large flight muscles … without moving their wings'. The other options are not mentioned.
