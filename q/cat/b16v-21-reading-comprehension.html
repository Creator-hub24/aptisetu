Passage: Minds in the sea
Minds in the sea

The octopus is an awkward animal for anyone who believes that intelligence is a mark of our own lineage. Its last common ancestor with humans was a small, probably worm-like creature that lived more than five hundred million years ago, long before either line had anything resembling a brain. Yet octopuses solve puzzles, use objects as tools, recognise individual people, and appear to play. Whatever intelligence they have, it evolved on its own, by a different route, in a body that could hardly be less like ours.

That body shapes the mind it carries. Most of an octopus's neurons are not in its central brain but in its arms, each of which can taste, touch and respond to what it encounters with a degree of independence. An arm severed from the body will still reach for food and attempt to pass it to a mouth that is no longer there. It is unclear what, if anything, it is like to be such an animal, but it is unlikely to resemble the single, centred point of view that we take for granted in ourselves.

These facts have become practical as well as philosophical. Proposals to farm octopuses on an industrial scale have prompted scientists and ethicists to ask whether the animals can suffer, and if so, what farming would mean for them. A government review in one country concluded that there was strong evidence of sentience in octopuses and their relatives, enough to bring them within animal-welfare law. The evidence included signs that injured octopuses tend their wounds, avoid places where they were hurt, and will accept a painkiller when one is offered.

Critics of the review pointed out that none of these behaviours proves that the animal feels anything. A sufficiently complicated machine might avoid damage and tend its injuries without any experience at all. This is true, but it proves less than the critics hope. The same could be said of any animal other than oneself; we infer that other mammals feel pain from just such behaviour, together with similarities of anatomy. In the octopus the anatomical similarity is missing, which makes the inference weaker, but the behavioural evidence is no less striking.

What, then, should follow from uncertainty? One view holds that we should extend protection only when sentience is established. The opposite view, increasingly influential, holds that when there is a realistic possibility that a creature can suffer, the burden of proof should fall on those who would subject it to conditions likely to cause suffering. The second view does not claim to know what the octopus feels. It claims that ignorance, in a matter where the possible harm is grave and the cost of caution modest, is a reason for restraint rather than a licence.

The octopus thus poses a test less of our science than of our habits of reasoning. We are used to extending moral concern along lines of resemblance. An animal whose mind may be rich but is certainly strange invites us to ask whether resemblance was ever the right guide.

Correct answer: C. much of the octopus's nervous control is distributed in its arms rather than centralised

Solution: The example follows 'Most of an octopus's neurons are not in its central brain but in its arms, each of which can … respond … with a degree of independence.' Regrowth, pain and brain size are not what the detail shows.
