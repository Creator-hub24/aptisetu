Passage: Shifting baselines in fisheries

A fisherman who began work forty years ago will often say that the sea was richer when he was young. His son, who began twenty years later, says the same of his own youth, though the catches he remembers as abundant were already smaller than his father's. Each generation takes as normal the state of the ecosystem it first encountered and measures decline from there. Over several generations the reference point drifts downwards, and a depleted sea comes to look natural to those who have never known anything else.

Ecologists call this the shifting baseline, and it affects scientists as much as fishermen. Systematic records of fish populations in many regions began only after industrial fishing had already reduced them substantially. A stock assessed as healthy because it has returned to its level of the 1970s may be a fraction of what it was a century earlier. Targets for recovery are set by reference to the data available, and the data available begin, inconveniently, at a point when much of the damage had already been done.

Historical sources can sometimes push the baseline back. Market records, tax ledgers, ships' logs and even restaurant menus have been used to estimate catches and the sizes of fish landed in earlier centuries. Such sources are fragmentary and were not collected for scientific purposes, so their numbers must be interpreted with care. A decline in the average size of fish recorded in market sales may reflect changes in taste, regulation or the gear used, rather than changes in the population. Yet when many independent sources point in the same direction, as they often do, the cumulative picture can be persuasive: many seas once held far larger and more numerous fish than any living person has seen.

The implications are not only scientific. Policies that aim to restore fish stocks must choose a target, and the choice of baseline determines how ambitious the target is. A recovery to the levels of a few decades ago may be achievable within a generation; a recovery to pre-industrial levels may be impossible, since the ecosystem may have changed in ways that cannot be reversed. Choosing the more modest target risks accepting a degraded state as success. Choosing the more ambitious one risks setting goals that no policy can meet, which may discredit the effort altogether.

There is no formula for resolving this choice, but the concept of the shifting baseline at least makes it visible. A target that would have been called modest by an earlier generation of fishermen should not be presented as a return to nature. Being explicit about which past one is trying to restore, and why, is a precondition for honest debate about what restoration can mean.

The author would most likely agree that a policy aiming to restore fish stocks to their 1970s levels should:
Abe stated openly as a choice of one historical reference point among others, with its limitations acknowledged
Bbe abandoned as hopelessly unambitious
Cbe presented as a full return to natural conditions
Dbe replaced by a target of pre-industrial abundance in every case
Show answer & solution

Correct answer: A. be stated openly as a choice of one historical reference point among others, with its limitations acknowledged

Solution: The final paragraph: a modest target "should not be presented as a return to nature", and one should be explicit "about which past one is trying to restore". Option C contradicts this. Options B and D ignore the two-sided risk described in para 4.
