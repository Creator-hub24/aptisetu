Passage: Monsoon forecasting and its users

Forecasts of the monsoon are among the most consequential predictions issued anywhere. Hundreds of millions of farmers depend on its rains, and decisions about what to sow, when to sow and how much to borrow are made in its shadow. Over the past few decades, forecasters have improved their seasonal predictions considerably, drawing on better observations of ocean temperatures and more powerful models. Yet the benefits of better forecasts have reached farmers unevenly, and the reasons have less to do with meteorology than with how forecasts are expressed and used.

A seasonal forecast is a statement about probabilities over a large region. It may say that rainfall over a zone covering many districts is likely to be near normal, with a stated chance of being above or below. To a farmer deciding whether to plant a crop that needs steady rain in July, such a statement is of limited use. What matters to her is whether rain will fall on her fields in the weeks after sowing, and a forecast of near-normal seasonal totals is compatible with a long dry spell at precisely the wrong moment. The forecast can be accurate by its own standards and useless for her purposes.

The language of probability creates further problems. A forecast that gives a seventy per cent chance of normal rainfall will be wrong, in the sense that the less likely outcome occurs, about three times in ten. Farmers who acted on the forecast and suffered may conclude that forecasts are unreliable, when in fact the forecast performed exactly as a probability should. Communicating uncertainty honestly can thus erode trust, while communicating false certainty builds trust until the first conspicuous failure destroys it.

Some programmes have tried to close the gap by translating forecasts into advice. Rather than reporting probabilities, they tell farmers in a district that, given the outlook, an early-maturing variety is a safer choice, or that it would be prudent to delay sowing until a certain amount of rain has fallen. Advice of this kind can be acted upon, and studies suggest that farmers who receive it make better decisions on average. But advice embeds judgements about what risks a farmer should accept, and a farmer who could afford to gamble on a high-yielding crop might reasonably make a different choice from one who could not survive a failed harvest.

The broader lesson is that information is not useful merely by being accurate. Its value depends on its fit with the decisions of those who receive it: the scale at which they act, the time at which they must commit, and the risks they can bear. A forecasting service judged only by its skill scores may improve steadily while its users remain no better off. Measuring its success by what users do differently, and with what results, is harder but closer to the point.

Correct answer: C. a probabilistic forecast that turns out "wrong" in a given year may still have been well calibrated

Solution: The less likely outcome occurring three times in ten is "exactly as a probability should" perform. Option B is the approach shown to fail eventually. Options A and D are unsupported.
