Passage: The insurer's dilemma in a warming world

Insurance works by pooling risks that are individually unpredictable but collectively stable. No one knows whether a particular house will burn down this year, but an insurer covering a million houses can estimate with some confidence how many will, and set premiums accordingly. The arrangement depends on two conditions: that the past is a reasonable guide to the near future, and that losses in one place are not too closely linked to losses in another. A changing climate threatens both.

The first condition is weakened when the frequency of floods, fires and storms shifts faster than the records used to price them. An insurer relying on fifty years of claims data will underestimate risks that have grown in the last ten, and the error will be largest precisely where change is fastest. Models that project future hazards can correct for this in principle, but they carry uncertainty of their own, and insurers who price according to them may find themselves charging more than competitors who rely on the past. In a competitive market, the firm that is right too early can lose its customers to firms that are wrong.

The second condition is threatened when disasters grow larger. A wildfire that destroys an entire town, or a hurricane that floods a region, produces losses that are correlated rather than independent. Pooling offers less protection against such events, because the many policies in the pool suffer together. Insurers manage correlated risk by buying reinsurance and by holding capital in reserve, but both become more expensive as the scale of possible losses rises, and the cost is passed on to policyholders.

In the most exposed areas, the result has been a familiar sequence. Premiums rise, coverage narrows, and eventually some insurers withdraw. Homeowners who cannot obtain private insurance turn to state-backed schemes, which were usually designed as a last resort for a small number of hard cases and now find themselves covering a growing share of the market. Such schemes often charge less than the risk would justify, because raising prices sharply is politically difficult, and the shortfall is borne by taxpayers or by surcharges on policies elsewhere.

This has a consequence that is easy to overlook. A premium is not merely a cost; it is a signal. A rising premium tells a homeowner, a buyer and a local government that a place has become more dangerous, and it creates an incentive to build differently, to move or to invest in protection. When public schemes suppress that signal, they also blunt the incentive, and development may continue in places where the risk has outgrown it. Keeping insurance affordable is a reasonable aim, but pursued by holding prices down, it can encourage the very exposure that makes insurance unaffordable in the first place.

The statement "A premium is not merely a cost; it is a signal" serves to:
Aexplain how insurers calculate premiums
Bintroduce the idea that suppressing premiums can weaken incentives to reduce exposure to risk
Cshow that homeowners ignore the price of insurance
Dargue that premiums should always be as high as possible
Show answer & solution

Correct answer: B. introduce the idea that suppressing premiums can weaken incentives to reduce exposure to risk

Solution: The sentence opens the argument that a suppressed signal "blunt[s] the incentive". Options D and C are unsupported, and option A is not the paragraph's subject.
