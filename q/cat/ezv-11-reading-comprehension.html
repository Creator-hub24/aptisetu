Passage: Trees on city streets

Walk down a city street on a hot afternoon and you can feel the difference a row of trees makes. Measurements in many cities show that shaded pavements can be several degrees cooler than exposed ones, and the air under a dense canopy is noticeably more comfortable. Trees cool a street in two ways. Their leaves block sunlight before it can heat the road and walls, and they release water vapour, which carries heat away as it evaporates.

The benefits do not stop at temperature. Tree roots and the soil around them soak up rainwater that would otherwise rush into drains, which helps during heavy storms. Leaves trap some dust from traffic. Studies of shopping streets have found that people tend to linger longer where there is greenery, which is good news for small shops. There is also evidence that residents of leafy neighbourhoods report less stress, though such findings are harder to measure precisely. Birds and insects also find food and shelter in street trees, so a well-planted avenue can link small parks into a chain of green space across the city.

Yet street trees lead difficult lives. Their roots are squeezed between pipes, cables and foundations, and the soil beneath a pavement is often packed so hard that water and air cannot reach them. Many young trees planted with great ceremony die within a few years because nobody waters them during their first dry seasons. Others are cut back severely when they grow into overhead wires or block shop signs. A tree that survives all this may still be removed when a road is widened.

For these reasons, experts increasingly argue that the number of trees planted is a poor measure of success. A city that plants a thousand saplings and loses most of them has achieved little. What matters is how many trees reach maturity, because a large old tree provides many times the shade and water absorption of a young one. This shifts attention from planting days to less glamorous work: preparing good soil pits, choosing species that suit local conditions, and paying crews to water and inspect trees for years afterwards.

None of this is especially costly compared with other city spending, but it requires patience. The rewards of a tree arrive slowly, often after the officials who planted it have left office. Cities that have built lasting canopies tend to be those that treated trees as long-term public infrastructure, like water pipes or roads, rather than as decorations to be added for a ribbon-cutting.

According to the passage, how do trees cool a street?
ABy blocking sunlight and releasing water vapour that carries heat away.
BBy drawing cool air up from underground pipes.
CBy absorbing traffic noise and dust.
DBy preventing people from walking on hot pavements.
Show answer & solution

Correct answer: A. By blocking sunlight and releasing water vapour that carries heat away.

Solution: Paragraph 1 names the two ways: leaves block sunlight, and they release water vapour that removes heat as it evaporates. Option C mixes in a different benefit (dust) and noise is not mentioned. Options D and B are invented.
