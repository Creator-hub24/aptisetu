Passage: What a city tree is worth

Ask a city council why it plants trees and you will usually hear a list of services: shade that cools the street, leaves that trap dust, roots that soak up storm water, and branches that give birds somewhere to live. Each of these benefits can be measured, and some cities now publish reports that put a money value on their trees, adding up the electricity saved on air-conditioning and the drainage costs avoided. The totals are impressive, often running into large sums for a single city, and they have helped tree budgets survive in years when other spending was cut.

There is a risk in this way of arguing, however. Once a tree is defended as a service provider, it can be judged like any other provider, and replaced if a cheaper one appears. A drainage engineer may point out that an underground tank handles storm water more reliably than roots. A shade sail is cheaper to install than a tree is to raise. On a narrow spreadsheet, the tree begins to lose.

The spreadsheet also struggles with time. A newly planted sapling provides almost none of the listed services; a mature tree provides them in abundance. The value of the young tree lies almost entirely in a future that the yearly budget does not see. When money is short, it is tempting to cut the maintenance that keeps saplings alive, since the loss will not show up in the accounts for decades. Many cities have planted generously and then watched a large share of their new trees die within a few years.

None of this is an argument against measurement. Numbers have rescued many trees, and a council that knows how much its canopy is worth makes better decisions than one that does not. The point is that the numbers capture only the part of a tree's value that resembles a utility. People also value trees for reasons that resist pricing: the sense of seasons changing on an ordinary street, the memory of a particular tree outside a childhood home, the feeling that a neighbourhood is cared for. These are not soft extras, to be mentioned once the serious accounting is done. They are often what residents mention first when a tree is threatened.

A sensible policy would use the numbers as a floor rather than a ceiling, a minimum case that any tree can make for itself, while leaving room for the reasons that cannot be counted.

What does the author mean by using the numbers 'as a floor rather than a ceiling'?
ATrees should be valued only by their measurable services.
BMeasured services should set the minimum case for a tree, with unmeasurable values added on top.
CCouncils should spend as little as possible on trees.
DTree budgets should never be cut below a fixed level.
Show answer & solution

Correct answer: B. Measured services should set the minimum case for a tree, with unmeasurable values added on top.

Solution: A floor is a minimum, a ceiling a maximum. The sentence adds 'a minimum case … while leaving room for the reasons that cannot be counted'. Option A treats the numbers as a ceiling, the opposite.
