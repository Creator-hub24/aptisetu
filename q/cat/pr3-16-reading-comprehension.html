Passage: The life of scripts

A script is often thought of as a neutral tool for recording a language, chosen for its convenience and replaceable by any other that does the job. History suggests otherwise. Scripts carry religious, political and cultural associations, and changes of script have frequently been occasions of intense conflict. A single language may be written in different scripts by communities that wish to mark themselves as distinct, and two closely related languages may be kept apart, in their speakers' minds, largely by the fact that they are written differently.

The connection between script and identity has practical consequences. A government that changes the official script of a language cuts its citizens off, within a generation, from much of what was written before. Older books, documents and inscriptions become legible only to specialists, and the past is mediated by those who choose what to transcribe. Reformers who have made such changes have sometimes intended exactly this effect, presenting a new script as a break with a past they wished to leave behind. Others have pursued efficiency or literacy and discovered the cultural costs only later.

Arguments about efficiency are themselves less straightforward than they appear. An alphabetic script with a small number of letters is often said to be easier to learn than a script with thousands of characters. In a narrow sense this is true, since a learner can begin reading sooner. But a script that represents meaning more directly may allow speakers of mutually unintelligible varieties to read the same texts, and a script that fits the sound structure of one dialect may serve others poorly. What counts as efficient depends on what the script is expected to do and for whom.

Digital technology has added a new layer to these questions. A script that is not supported by the standard encoding systems, or for which fonts and keyboards are scarce, is at a disadvantage in a world where much writing happens on screens. Communities whose scripts were poorly supported in the early decades of computing often adopted another script for digital use, or wrote their language in Latin letters in ways that varied from person to person. Support has since improved for many scripts, but habits formed in the interim have proved durable. A generation that learned to send messages in improvised spellings does not easily return to a script it seldom used on a screen.

These pressures do not all point in the same direction. Technology can marginalise a script, but it can also revive one, by making it cheap to produce printed materials, fonts and learning tools for communities that could never have supported a traditional publishing industry. Whether a script flourishes may depend less on its intrinsic merits than on the institutions willing to sustain it, and on whether its users regard it as worth the effort of keeping.

The fourth paragraph primarily serves to:
Aargue that Latin letters should replace all other scripts
Bshow that technology has had only beneficial effects on scripts
Cexplain the history of encoding standards in detail
Dintroduce digital support as an additional factor affecting how scripts are used, with lasting effects from earlier gaps
Show answer & solution

Correct answer: D. introduce digital support as an additional factor affecting how scripts are used, with lasting effects from earlier gaps

Solution: It opens "Digital technology has added a new layer" and notes that interim habits "have proved durable". Option B contradicts the disadvantage it describes. Options A and C are unsupported.
