Passage: How railways set the clock

Until the middle of the nineteenth century, every town kept its own time. Noon was the moment the sun stood highest over the local church or town hall, and clocks were set by it. Because the sun appears to move from east to west, a town a few hundred kilometres to the west reached noon several minutes later than its neighbour. For most people this hardly mattered. A farmer walking to market did not care whether his clock agreed with one in a distant city.

The railways changed that. A train could now cover in a morning a distance that once took days, and its timetable had to be read by passengers at many stations. If each station printed departures in its own local time, the same train seemed to leave at odd and confusing minutes, and travellers who changed lines often missed their connections. Worse, railway staff working on single-track lines relied on timetables to keep trains apart. A disagreement of a few minutes between two stations could put two engines on the same stretch of track.

Railway companies responded by choosing one time for the whole network, usually the time at a major city, and telling every station to follow it. In Britain this 'railway time' spread quickly in the 1840s, carried along the telegraph wires that ran beside the tracks. Some towns resisted at first. A few public clocks were fitted with two minute hands, one for local time and one for the railway, so that citizens could see what they were giving up. Within a generation, however, the convenience of a single time won, and the law eventually followed the practice.

Larger countries faced a harder problem, because one time across a whole continent would put noon in the middle of the night somewhere. The answer, agreed in stages during the late nineteenth century, was to divide the world into zones, each usually one hour apart. Inside a zone, clocks agree; crossing into the next zone, travellers simply move their watches forward or back by an hour.

Time zones now feel so natural that it is easy to forget they were invented for a practical reason. They were not handed down by astronomers seeking accuracy. If anything, they made clocks less faithful to the local sun. They were a compromise, accepted because a society that moves quickly needs its members to agree on what time it is, even if that agreed time is slightly wrong for the place where they stand.

Which of the following best captures the main idea of the passage?
AStandard time was a practical agreement that faster travel made necessary, even though it departs from local solar time.
BBritain was the first country in the world to build railways.
CRailways were dangerous in the nineteenth century because of poor engineering.
DLocal solar time is more accurate than any other way of keeping time and should be restored.
Show answer & solution

Correct answer: A. Standard time was a practical agreement that faster travel made necessary, even though it departs from local solar time.

Solution: The passage traces how faster rail travel forced towns to agree on one time, then zones, and ends by calling this a useful compromise. Option C picks one detail (safety) and adds 'poor engineering'. Option B is not claimed. Option D takes a position the author does not take.
