Passage: Telling more than we can know
Telling more than we can know

Ask people why they chose one of several identical pairs of stockings laid out on a table, and they will give reasons readily: this pair felt softer, that one had a better sheen. In a well-known set of experiments, the pairs really were identical, and the choice was driven mostly by position: people tended to prefer whichever item lay furthest to the right. Yet almost nobody mentioned position, and when it was suggested to them, most denied it firmly. They were not lying. They sincerely believed the reasons they gave.

Findings of this kind have led some psychologists to a startling conclusion: that we have far less direct access to the causes of our own behaviour than we suppose. On this view, when we explain our choices we are not reading off a report from some inner observatory. We are doing what an outsider would do: drawing on a stock of plausible theories about why people act as they do, and applying the one that seems to fit. The tell-tale sign is that our explanations of our own behaviour are often no more accurate than the explanations a stranger would offer for us, and are wrong in the same ways.

It would be easy to overstate the point. The experiments show that people can be mistaken about causes they have no reason to notice, such as the position of an item on a table. They do not show that we are wrong about everything inside us. A person in pain generally knows that she is in pain, and someone working through a sum in her head can usually say which step she is on. What the research undermines is not self-knowledge in general but a particular confidence: the belief that when we can produce a reason for an action, the reason we produce is the one that moved us.

That confidence matters, because so much of social life depends on the reasons people give. We ask job candidates why they want the post, witnesses why they noticed what they did, and voters why they prefer one candidate. If the answers are partly constructions, assembled after the fact from the stock of available theories, then they tell us less about the person than we assume, and more about what that person, and the culture around her, regards as a respectable reason.

There is a further and less comfortable implication. If our explanations are theories, they can be improved, as theories can, but only by evidence that comes from outside. The person who wishes to know why she really avoids certain colleagues may learn more from a careful record of when she does so than from any amount of reflection. Introspection, on this account, is not useless, but it is a source of hypotheses rather than of verdicts, and it deserves the same scepticism we bring to any hypothesis that flatters the person who holds it.

Show answer & solution

Correct answer: B. people can sincerely give reasons for a choice that were not its actual cause

Solution: The choice 'was driven mostly by position', yet people cited softness or sheen and 'sincerely believed the reasons they gave'. 'Deliberately conceal' contradicts 'They were not lying'. The passage offers no explanation of the right-hand preference, and the point is not about telling products apart: the pairs were identical.
