Passage: Sleep and the cleaning of the brain

Why animals sleep has long been one of biology's more embarrassing open questions. Sleep is costly: a sleeping animal cannot feed, mate or watch for predators, and yet almost every animal studied sleeps in some form, from fruit flies to whales. A behaviour so widespread and so risky must serve some important function, but the candidates proposed over the past century, from conserving energy to consolidating memory, have each explained part of the evidence and left much of it unexplained.

One of the more recent proposals concerns waste. The brain is metabolically active and produces by-products that must be removed, including proteins that, when they accumulate, are associated with degenerative disease. Experiments in mice suggested that the flow of fluid through the brain, which carries such waste away, increases markedly during sleep, as the spaces between brain cells widen. On this view, sleep is in part a period of maintenance, when the brain does work that is difficult to combine with the demands of waking activity.

The proposal attracted wide attention, partly because it offered a simple, physical answer to a question that had resisted simple answers, and partly because it seemed to link poor sleep with conditions that are feared and poorly understood. It also prompted careful scrutiny. Later studies using different methods questioned whether clearance actually increases during sleep, and some found the opposite. The disagreement turns partly on technique: measuring fluid flow in a living brain is difficult, and the anaesthetics used in some experiments may themselves alter the processes being measured.

Even if clearance does increase during sleep, it would not follow that this is why sleep exists. A function can be served by a behaviour without being the reason the behaviour evolved; a process that takes advantage of an existing period of inactivity is different from one that required it. The waste hypothesis would be stronger if it could explain features of sleep that other theories cannot, such as why sleep deprivation is eventually fatal, or why the need for sleep varies between species in the way it does. It would also need to explain why clearance could not simply proceed more slowly during waking hours, as many other forms of bodily maintenance do. The kidneys and liver clean the blood continuously without requiring the animal to lie still for a third of its life, and a theory that makes the brain an exception owes an account of what is special about it.

The history of the question suggests caution about any single answer. Sleep may have originated for one reason and acquired other functions over time, so that it now serves several purposes, none of which alone explains it. If so, the search for the function of sleep may have been misconceived from the start, and the more productive question is what sleep does in a particular species and how those uses fit together.

Which of the following findings would most weaken the waste-clearance hypothesis as an explanation of why sleep exists?
AMice that are deprived of sleep show accumulation of certain proteins.
BSome species with very low metabolic rates in the brain nonetheless sleep as much as species with high rates.
CAnaesthetics alter many brain processes.
DFluid flow in the brain varies with body position.
Show answer & solution

Correct answer: B. Some species with very low metabolic rates in the brain nonetheless sleep as much as species with high rates.

Solution: If sleep exists to clear metabolic waste, need for sleep should track waste production. Species with little brain metabolism sleeping as much (option B) cuts against that. Option A supports the hypothesis. Options D and C concern measurement, not the explanatory claim.
