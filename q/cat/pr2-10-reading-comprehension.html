Passage: Constitutions and the dead hand

A written constitution binds future generations to choices made by people who are no longer alive. This feature has troubled democratic theorists since constitutions were first written down. If the people are sovereign, why should the living be governed by rules they did not make and can change only with great difficulty? One famous answer was that each generation should rewrite its constitution afresh. The proposal has never been adopted, but the objection behind it has never been fully answered.

Defenders of entrenchment usually make two arguments. The first is that constitutions protect the people from themselves: majorities, in moments of passion or fear, may wish to suppress rights or concentrate power, and rules that are hard to change slow them down. The second is that stable rules allow citizens and governments to plan. A political order in which basic arrangements could be overturned by any passing majority would leave everyone uncertain about what the next election might bring. Investors, minorities and opposition parties alike have reason to value rules that will still be in force after a change of government, and a constitution that offered no such assurance would encourage each faction to seize what it could while it held power.

Both arguments have force, but neither shows that entrenchment is democratic in any simple sense. The first frankly limits majorities; it is defensible, but its defence is that some things matter more than majority rule. The second shows that stability is valuable, but not that it should be secured by the decisions of a particular generation rather than by some other means. A constitution could, in principle, be stable without being difficult to amend, if its rules were widely accepted.

In practice, the tension is managed less by formal amendment than by interpretation. Courts reading an old text apply its general phrases to circumstances its authors could not have imagined, and in doing so they adjust the constitution to the present without changing a word. This makes the document more flexible than its amendment rules suggest, but it shifts the question of democratic legitimacy from the dead authors to the living judges. Critics who object to the dead hand of the founders may find they have exchanged it for the living hand of an unelected court.

The choice is therefore not between a constitution that binds and one that does not, but between different ways of distributing the power to adapt fundamental law. Some systems make amendment relatively easy and expect legislatures to keep the text current; others make it hard and rely on courts; still others hold periodic conventions. Each places the power to change basic rules in different hands, and each carries its own risks: instability, judicial overreach or the capture of conventions by organised minorities. A constitution that does not change at all is not among the realistic options, since circumstances change whether or not the text does.
