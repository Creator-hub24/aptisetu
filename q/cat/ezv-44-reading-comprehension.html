Passage: Why we keep a spare key

Most households keep a spare key somewhere: with a neighbour, under a flowerpot, or in a drawer at a relative's house. The spare is rarely used. Yet people who lose their only key, and spend an evening waiting for a locksmith, tend to make a spare the very next day. The spare key is a small example of a larger idea that engineers call redundancy: having more than one way of doing something important, so that a single failure does not stop everything.

Redundancy is everywhere in systems that must not fail. Aircraft have more than one engine and several independent ways of controlling the wings. Hospitals keep generators in case the electricity supply is cut. Large websites copy their data onto machines in different cities. In each case, the extra part sits idle most of the time, and an accountant looking only at everyday costs might call it waste. The value of the spare shows only in the rare moment when the main part fails. Then the backup takes over, often so smoothly that passengers, patients or users never notice that anything went wrong.

That is exactly the temptation organisations face. When budgets are tight, spare capacity is an easy target. A factory can save money by holding less stock in its warehouse and relying on deliveries arriving just in time. A team can work with no extra staff, so that everyone is fully busy. In normal times these choices look efficient. The cost appears only when something goes wrong: a delivery is delayed, a worker falls ill, and there is nothing in reserve to absorb the shock.

This does not mean that more redundancy is always better. Every spare costs money, takes space and must be maintained. A spare generator that has never been tested may fail on the one day it is needed. The skill lies in deciding which failures would be serious enough to justify a backup, and in checking that the backup really works.

The spare key captures this balance well. It is cheap, and the trouble it saves is large, so almost everyone agrees it is worth having. But it only helps if someone remembers where it is and if it still fits the lock. Good redundancy, in a household or a hospital, is not just a matter of owning a spare. It means keeping that spare ready for the day it is needed.

Which of the following best summarises the passage?
ASpare capacity can look wasteful in normal times but protects against failure, provided it is chosen sensibly and kept ready.
BSpare keys are the most important safety measure in a household.
COrganisations should always cut spare capacity to save money.
DEvery system should have as many backups as possible.
Show answer & solution

Correct answer: A. Spare capacity can look wasteful in normal times but protects against failure, provided it is chosen sensibly and kept ready.

Solution: This combines the passage's main points: redundancy looks like waste (paragraphs 2 and 3), it protects against shocks, it should be chosen sensibly (paragraph 4), and it must be kept ready (paragraph 5). Option C is the temptation the author warns against. Option D is rejected in paragraph 4. Option B overstates the illustration.
