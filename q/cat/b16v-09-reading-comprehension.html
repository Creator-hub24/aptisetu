Passage: The invention of wilderness
The invention of wilderness

The great national parks of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries were founded on a picture of nature as a place without people. Their creators spoke of untouched landscapes, of country as it had been before human history began, and they drew boundaries to keep it that way. What they seldom acknowledged was that many of these landscapes had been inhabited, hunted, burned and managed for thousands of years, and that making them empty required removing the people who lived there.

Historians have since shown how much of the 'wilderness' that visitors admire is the product of that management. Open grasslands that early travellers took for natural features were often maintained by regular, deliberate burning; when the burning stopped after the inhabitants were expelled, forests crept in and the very scenery the parks had been created to protect began to change. In some places, the removal of grazing or of small-scale cultivation reduced rather than increased the variety of plants and animals.

None of this means that the founders were simply wrong to want to protect these places. The threats they saw, from logging, mining and settlement on an industrial scale, were real, and the parks did save landscapes that would otherwise have been destroyed. The error lay in the idea that protection meant the absence of people, and in the assumption that the people who were there did not count as part of what was being protected.

The idea persists in subtler forms. Conservation funding often flows more readily to projects that promise to keep areas 'intact', a word that quietly implies that human presence is a form of damage. Communities living inside or beside protected areas may be allowed to stay only on condition that they give up practices, such as seasonal burning or the collection of forest produce, that may have helped to shape the habitat in the first place. In such cases, conservation can end up undermining the very ecological conditions it aims to preserve.

The alternative is not to abolish protected areas but to rethink what they are for. If many valued landscapes are joint products of natural processes and long human use, then protecting them may require keeping some of that use going, and the people who know how to do it may be the most useful allies a park can have. Some parks have begun to work in this way, restoring traditional burning under the guidance of the communities that once practised it.

The lesson reaches beyond particular parks. The word 'wilderness' carries a story about human beings as outsiders to nature, visitors who can only spoil what they touch. That story has inspired admirable acts of protection. But a story in which people are always the problem makes it hard to imagine them as part of any solution, and it has fallen hardest on those whose presence in the landscape was longest.

According to the passage, the 'error' of the park founders lay in:
Aequating protection with the absence of people and disregarding the inhabitants as part of what was protected
Btrying to protect landscapes that were not in fact threatened
Callowing logging and mining inside the parks
Dfailing to draw boundaries around the parks
Show answer & solution

Correct answer: A. equating protection with the absence of people and disregarding the inhabitants as part of what was protected

Solution: Para 3: 'The error lay in the idea that protection meant the absence of people, and in the assumption that the people who were there did not count as part of what was being protected.' The same paragraph says the threats 'were real', and the founders did draw boundaries.
