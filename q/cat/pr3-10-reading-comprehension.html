Passage: The puzzle of cooperation among strangers

Human beings cooperate with unrelated strangers on a scale unknown in other animals. We pay taxes that support people we will never meet, follow rules when no one is watching, and contribute to causes whose benefits we will not share. For evolutionary biologists this is a puzzle, because natural selection is usually expected to favour individuals who take the benefits of cooperation without paying its costs. Explanations for cooperation among relatives, or among individuals who interact repeatedly and can return favours, are well established. Cooperation among strangers who will not meet again is harder to explain.

One family of explanations emphasises reputation. Even if I will never meet a particular stranger again, others may observe how I treat him, and my reputation for fairness may bring me partners and benefits in the future. Experiments show that people behave more generously when they believe they are being watched, and even an image of eyes on a wall can increase contributions to an honesty box. On this view cooperation among strangers is an extension of reciprocity: the return comes not from the person helped but from the wider community that observed the help.

A second family emphasises norms and their enforcement. Human groups develop rules about fair behaviour and punish those who violate them, sometimes at a cost to the punisher. Experiments in which participants can pay to reduce the earnings of those who behave unfairly find that many are willing to do so, even when they gain nothing. If enough people punish violators, cooperation becomes the safer strategy, and the norms become self-sustaining. The puzzle then shifts from cooperation to punishment, since the punisher bears a cost for a benefit shared by all.

A third account appeals to competition between groups. Groups whose members cooperate may outcompete groups whose members do not, and if the differences between groups persist, through culture rather than genes, cooperative practices may spread even if they are costly to individuals within each group. Critics argue that the conditions required for such group-level selection are restrictive and that the evidence for them is disputed. Groups must differ consistently in their practices, those differences must outlast migration and intermarriage, and competition between groups must be intense enough to outweigh the advantage that free riders enjoy within each one.

The three accounts are often presented as rivals, but they need not be. Reputation makes punishment cheaper, since a person known to punish cheats may be cheated less often; norms specify what counts as a good reputation; and competition between groups may favour those whose norms and reputational systems work best. The more interesting question may be not which mechanism explains human cooperation, but how they came to reinforce one another in a species whose capacity for language allowed reputations to travel far beyond the people who witnessed an act.

Correct answer: A. explaining cooperation through costly punishment raises the question of why individuals bear the cost of punishing for a shared benefit

Solution: Since "the punisher bears a cost for a benefit shared by all", the original free-rider puzzle reappears at the level of punishment. Option B is not claimed. Options C and D contradict the paragraph.
