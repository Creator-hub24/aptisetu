Passage: The second life of clothes
The second life of clothes

Every year, bales of used clothing donated in wealthy countries are sorted, compressed and shipped to markets in East and West Africa, where traders buy them unseen and resell the contents piece by piece. For the donor the transaction ends at the collection bin and feels like charity. For the economies at the other end it is something more complicated: a large, unregulated trade that has been blamed for destroying local textile industries and praised for clothing millions of people cheaply.

The case against the trade is usually made through history. In the decades after independence, several countries built clothing and textile factories that employed tens of thousands of workers. Many of those factories have since closed, and their decline coincided with a flood of imported second-hand clothing. Critics draw the obvious conclusion and some governments have acted on it, proposing high tariffs or outright bans on used garments in the hope of reviving domestic production.

The conclusion is less obvious than it looks. The same decades saw other changes that hurt local factories at least as much: the removal of protective tariffs under structural adjustment programmes, unreliable electricity, and above all the arrival of very cheap new clothing from Asia. Studies that try to separate these causes generally find that second-hand imports played a real but partial role. A ban on used clothing, on this evidence, would be more likely to open the market to cheap new imports than to bring back the old factories.

Meanwhile the trade itself supports a dense web of livelihoods. Importers sell bales to wholesalers, who sell them to market traders, who employ tailors to alter, mend and restyle what they buy. Because no trader knows what a bale contains until it is opened, the business resembles a lottery in which skill lies in judging suppliers, spotting quality and repairing what others would discard. Those who make a living this way are not victims of the trade but its practitioners, and their interests are seldom heard in debates conducted on their behalf.

None of this makes the trade benign. The quality of bales has declined as fast fashion has spread in the donor countries, so that a growing share of what arrives is too worn or too flimsy to sell. That share ends up in landfills and on riverbanks in the importing countries, which bear the cost of disposing of clothes that the donors have, in effect, exported rather than thrown away. The problem here is not that Africans buy used clothes but that rich countries consume clothing at a rate their own waste systems would struggle to absorb.

A useful debate would separate these questions. Whether used clothing harms local industry is an empirical matter, and the answer appears to be: somewhat, but less than other forces. Whether the trade's waste is fair is a question about responsibility, and the answer points back towards the donors' wardrobes rather than towards the markets that receive their castoffs.

The comparison of the trade with 'a lottery' is meant to suggest that:
Atraders are gamblers who usually lose money
Bthe trade should be regulated like gambling
Ccustomers cannot know the quality of what they buy
Dsuccess in the trade depends on skill in managing uncertainty, not merely on chance
Show answer & solution

Correct answer: D. success in the trade depends on skill in managing uncertainty, not merely on chance

Solution: Right after the comparison: 'skill lies in judging suppliers, spotting quality and repairing what others would discard'. The uncertainty is the trader's, since no one knows a bale's contents until it is opened, and the point is that skill handles it. Losses, regulation, and customers' ignorance are not claimed.
