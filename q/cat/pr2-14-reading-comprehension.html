Passage: Photography and the evidence of the eye

When photography was invented, many commentators greeted it as the end of a long human difficulty: the unreliability of testimony. A painter could flatter, a witness could lie or misremember, but the camera, it seemed, simply recorded what was in front of it. Courts, newspapers and scientists adopted photographs as evidence, and the phrase "the camera does not lie" became a commonplace. It was never quite true, and the ways in which it was false are instructive.

The photographer chooses where to stand, when to press the shutter and what to leave outside the frame. A photograph of a crowded square taken from one angle may suggest a mass demonstration; another, taken a few metres away, may show a thin gathering against empty streets. Neither image is false in the sense of depicting something that did not exist, yet each conveys an impression that the other contradicts. The camera records faithfully what falls within its frame, and this faithfulness lends authority to choices made outside it.

Manipulation of the image itself is almost as old as the medium. Early photographers painted out unwanted details, combined negatives to produce scenes that never occurred and posed subjects to create the appearance of spontaneity. Such interventions required skill and time, and their products could often be detected by experts. Some fabrications nonetheless circulated for decades: pictures of spirits hovering behind sitters, of fairies at the bottom of a garden, of officials who had fallen from favour quietly removed from group portraits. That such images were believed says as much about the readiness of viewers to trust the medium as about the skill of those who exploited it. The authority of photography rested less on the impossibility of manipulation than on its cost: it was easier to photograph a scene than to fabricate one convincingly.

Digital tools have changed this balance. Images can now be altered or generated with little effort, and the result may be indistinguishable from a recording to any viewer who lacks the original files. The predictable response has been a call for technical safeguards: signatures embedded in images at the moment of capture, records of every subsequent edit. Such measures can help, but they shift trust from the image to the system that certifies it, and they work only if those systems are themselves trusted and widely used.

The deeper change may be in how images are read. For more than a century, viewers granted photographs a presumption of truth and required reasons to doubt them. That presumption is weakening, and some observers fear a world in which no image can be believed. Yet the older presumption was always too generous, as the history of framing and staging shows. A culture that asks of every image who made it, from where, and for what purpose may be better equipped than one that trusted the camera, even if it has lost the comfort of that trust.

The author's statement that faithfulness "lends authority to choices made outside it" implies that:
Aphotographers deliberately deceive their viewers
Bthe frame of a photograph is always misleading
Cphotographs taken from different angles are equally false
Dthe accuracy of what is recorded can make the photographer's selective decisions appear neutral
Show answer & solution

Correct answer: D. the accuracy of what is recorded can make the photographer's selective decisions appear neutral

Solution: Para 2: each image is faithful, yet the choice of angle shapes the impression, and faithfulness makes that choice seem authoritative. Option A imputes intent. Options B and C overstate.
