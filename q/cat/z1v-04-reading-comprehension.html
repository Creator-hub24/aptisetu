Passage: Libraries of things

Most households own an electric drill, yet the average drill is used for only a few minutes in its whole life. It spends years in a drawer, waiting for the one shelf that needs putting up. The same is true of ladders, pressure washers, camping tents and sewing machines. Each family buys its own, stores its own and eventually throws its own away, often while it still works. Multiply that by every street in a town and the result is a great deal of money, metal and plastic sitting idle in the dark.

A small but growing number of towns have tried a different arrangement: the library of things. It works much like a book library. Members pay a small yearly fee, or nothing at all, and borrow tools and equipment for a few days at a time. A volunteer checks each item when it comes back, charges the batteries and notes any damage. The collection usually starts with donations from people clearing out garages and grows as members suggest what they would like to borrow.

Supporters point to three benefits. The first is money: a family that borrows a carpet cleaner twice a year saves the price of buying one. The second is space, which matters in small city flats where a cupboard is precious. The third is waste. When fewer people buy rarely used items, fewer are made, shipped and finally dumped. Some libraries also run repair evenings, where volunteers show members how to fix a broken toaster instead of replacing it.

The idea has limits. A library of things works best where people live close together, because nobody wants to travel an hour to borrow a hammer. It also depends on trust. If borrowers return items late or broken, the collection shrinks and members drift away. Several early projects closed for exactly this reason. The ones that last tend to keep careful records, ask for a refundable deposit on costly items and, above all, build a sense that the collection belongs to the members rather than to some distant organisation.

Perhaps the most surprising lesson is that the libraries are not only about objects. Members often say that the best part is meeting neighbours they had never spoken to, swapping advice on how to use a tile cutter, or being asked to help with a project. In that sense a shelf of shared tools does what the town square once did: it gives people a reason to meet.

The author compares a shelf of shared tools to a town square in order to suggest that
Alibraries of things should be built in town squares.
Bthe libraries give neighbours a reason to meet and get to know each other.
Ctown squares are no longer useful in modern cities.
Dtools were once sold in town squares.
Show answer & solution

Correct answer: B. the libraries give neighbours a reason to meet and get to know each other.

Solution: The last paragraph says members value meeting neighbours, and the comparison is explained directly: 'it gives people a reason to meet.' Option A takes the comparison literally; options C and D are not said.
