Passage: The replication problem and its uses

When a large collaboration of psychologists attempted to repeat a hundred published experiments, fewer than half produced results resembling the originals. The finding was widely reported as a scandal, and in part it was one. Some of the original studies had relied on small samples, flexible analyses and the selective reporting of outcomes that happened to reach statistical significance. Practices that had seemed harmless individually, such as trying several ways of measuring an effect and reporting the one that worked, combined to fill the literature with results that were unlikely to be true.

Yet a failure to replicate is not, by itself, proof that the original result was false. An experiment is never repeated exactly. The participants differ, the setting differs, and small changes in procedure that seemed irrelevant to the replicating team may turn out to matter. Defenders of some original findings argued that their effects depended on conditions that the replications had not reproduced, and in a few cases later work supported them. In others the appeal to hidden conditions looked more like a refusal to accept bad news, since the conditions were specified only after the replication had failed.

The difficulty is that both kinds of response sound alike. A researcher who says that an effect appears only under certain circumstances may be describing a genuine feature of the phenomenon or protecting a cherished result, and there is no way to tell from the claim alone. What distinguishes the two is whether the circumstances were stated in advance and whether they generate new predictions that can be tested. An effect that is said to depend on the time of day, the mood of participants and the wording of instructions, with each condition named only after a failure, has become too flexible to be refuted, and a claim that cannot be refuted has ceased to tell us much about the world.

The episode has led to reforms. Many journals now encourage researchers to register their hypotheses and analysis plans before collecting data, so that the distinction between predictions and post hoc explanations is visible. Large collaborative studies, run across many laboratories at once, test whether an effect survives the variation that any single laboratory would miss. These reforms do not guarantee truth, but they make it harder for a field to deceive itself, which is perhaps the most that method can achieve.

There is a further lesson, less often drawn. The original findings that failed to replicate were not hidden; they were published in respected journals, cited widely and taught to students. The problem was not that the field lacked a mechanism for correction but that the mechanism worked slowly, because there was little reward for checking what had already been accepted. A science corrects itself only when some of its members are paid, promoted or praised for doing the correcting, and for much of the period in question they were not.

According to the author, an appeal to "hidden conditions" becomes suspect when:
Athe conditions are specified only after a failure and yield no new testable predictions
Bthe conditions concern the mood of participants
Cthe replication was conducted in a different country
Dthe original experiment used a large sample
Show answer & solution

Correct answer: A. the conditions are specified only after a failure and yield no new testable predictions

Solution: Para 3: what distinguishes genuine from protective claims is "whether the circumstances were stated in advance and whether they generate new predictions". Option B mistakes an example for the criterion. Options D and C are not criteria in the passage.
