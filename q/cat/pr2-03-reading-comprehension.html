Passage: The craft economy of the city

Writers on modern work often describe a choice between two futures. In one, automation and global trade hollow out manual occupations, leaving a divided economy of highly paid professionals and poorly paid service workers. In the other, technology frees people for more creative work. Both pictures share an assumption: that the skilled manual trades are a remnant of an older economy, destined to shrink as machines improve. A visit to almost any large city suggests that the assumption is at least incomplete.

Cities are full of work that resists both mechanisation and offshoring. The plumber, the electrician, the furniture restorer, the tailor who alters suits and the mechanic who repairs bicycles all perform tasks that require judgement applied to particular, irregular objects in particular places. A machine can make a thousand identical chairs; it cannot easily diagnose why a hundred-year-old chair wobbles, or repair it without destroying what makes it worth keeping. Nor can such work be shipped abroad, since the leaking pipe and the wobbling chair are here. The demand for these services has, if anything, grown as cities have become richer, because wealthy households own more things and have less time to maintain them.

What has changed is the status of the work rather than its existence. In many countries the trades were for a generation presented to young people as a fallback for those who could not succeed at school, and training systems were allowed to decline. The result in some places is a shortage of skilled tradespeople alongside a surplus of graduates whose qualifications the market values less than they were promised. Wages in some trades have risen accordingly, and it is no longer unusual for an experienced electrician to earn more than a junior professional in an office.

It would be a mistake to romanticise this. Much manual work is physically demanding and can be hard to sustain over a long career, and the self-employed tradesperson bears risks that the salaried employee does not: irregular income, no paid leave, the need to find customers continuously. The pleasures of craft are real but unevenly distributed, and they are more often described by those who write about the trades than by those who practise them under pressure.

Still, the persistence of the trades suggests that the usual accounts of the future of work are drawn from too narrow a sample. They describe what happens to tasks that can be standardised, and they extrapolate from these to work in general. A great deal of economic activity consists of dealing with things that are old, broken, unique or inconvenient, and such activity is likely to remain stubbornly local and human. A policy that prepares young people only for the work that machines are expected to leave behind in offices may neglect a large body of work that machines were never likely to take.

The fourth paragraph serves mainly to:
Aqualify the author's case by acknowledging the real hardships of manual and self-employed work
Bargue that the trades should be abolished
Cshow that writers about the trades are dishonest
Dprove that office work is safer than manual work
Show answer & solution

Correct answer: A. qualify the author's case by acknowledging the real hardships of manual and self-employed work

Solution: The paragraph opens "It would be a mistake to romanticise this" and lists physical demands and financial risks. Option C overstates the remark about writers. Options B and D are unsupported.
