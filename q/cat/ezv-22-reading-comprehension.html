Passage: What libraries are for now

When home internet connections became common, many people predicted that public libraries would fade away. If any fact could be found on a phone in seconds, why would anyone visit a building full of books? Some local governments acted on this belief and cut library budgets, shortened opening hours or closed branches altogether. The prediction seemed reasonable. It turned out to misunderstand what libraries actually do.

Libraries have never been only stores of information. They are also among the few indoor public places where a person can stay for hours without being expected to buy anything. Students without a quiet room at home use them to study. Elderly residents come for newspapers and company. Parents bring small children to story sessions, often the child's first experience of a group activity outside the family. Job seekers use library computers and printers to prepare applications, and many people still need help from a librarian to fill in an online form. On a cold or very hot day, a library may also be the only comfortable public room nearby. For people living alone, a short conversation at the front desk can be the main social contact of the week.

In fact, the internet has created new reasons to visit. Government services, banking and even school homework have moved online, but not everyone has a reliable connection, a working computer or the confidence to use them. For these people the library is the bridge. Librarians in many towns report that they spend less time finding books and more time helping visitors with passwords, email accounts and digital forms.

Libraries that have thrived tend to be those that recognised this shift. They have rearranged their spaces, adding study rooms, meeting areas and workshops alongside the shelves. Some lend tools, musical instruments or laptops. Others host classes in everything from basic computing to tax filing. Books remain central, and borrowing figures for children's books in particular have held up well, but they are no longer the whole story.

None of this means that libraries are safe. They depend on public funding, and their benefits are spread across many people in small amounts, which makes them easy to cut when budgets are tight. But the early prediction of their disappearance was based on a narrow view of their purpose. A library is less a warehouse of books than a shared room open to everyone, and the need for such rooms has not gone away.

Which of the following groups is NOT mentioned as using libraries?
ATourists looking for maps of the town.
BElderly residents looking for newspapers and company.
CStudents who need a quiet place to study.
DJob seekers preparing applications.
Show answer & solution

Correct answer: A. Tourists looking for maps of the town.

Solution: Paragraph 2 lists students, elderly residents, parents with children and job seekers. Tourists are never mentioned.
