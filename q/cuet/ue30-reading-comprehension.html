Passage: Factual passage: Stepwells
Factual passage: Stepwells

In the dry parts of western India, people once solved the problem of water not by building walls around it but by digging down to meet it. The result was the stepwell, known as a vav or baoli: a well so wide that a flight of steps could lead from the ground to the water below. As the water level rose and fell with the seasons, villagers simply walked down or up to reach it.

Stepwells were more than water tanks. The cool, shaded air at the bottom made them places to rest in the heat of the day, and travellers on long trade routes often stopped at them. Many were decorated with carved pillars, balconies and sculpted figures, because the people who paid for them saw the gift of water as an act of merit. Some, like Rani ki Vav in Gujarat, are now World Heritage sites.

Their decline began when piped water and hand pumps arrived. Once water could be drawn without walking down a hundred steps, many stepwells were abandoned, filled with rubbish or used as dumps. Yet some are being given new life. Volunteers have cleared silt, repaired walls and let rainwater flow back in. The lesson, they say, is practical as well as historical: structures that store rain where it falls may be useful again in regions where groundwater is running low.

What led to the decline of stepwells?
AThe arrival of piped water and hand pumps
BA shortage of stone for repairs
CLaws that banned their use
DHeavy rainfall that flooded them
Show answer & solution

Correct answer: A. The arrival of piped water and hand pumps

Solution: The third paragraph says the decline began when piped water and hand pumps arrived and water could be drawn without climbing down.
