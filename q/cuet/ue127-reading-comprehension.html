Passage: Literary passage: The last keeper
Literary passage: The last keeper

For thirty-one years Joseph Fernandes had climbed the hundred and twelve steps of the lighthouse every evening at dusk. He knew each step by its sound: the seventh rang slightly, the fortieth had a crack his boot always found, and the last ten groaned like an old man rising from a chair. At the top he would wipe the great lens, light the lamp and write the time in a ledger that nobody but the inspector ever read.

The letter from the department was polite. The light would be made automatic in March; a small machine would switch it on and off by itself, and a technician would visit twice a year. His service, it said, had been "much appreciated". Joseph read it twice, folded it into a careful square and put it under the clock.

On his last evening his granddaughter came with him. She counted the steps aloud and complained halfway up. At the top she pressed her face to the glass and watched the beam sweep out across the dark water, catching for an instant the white edge of a fishing boat.

"Does the machine know about the boats?" she asked.

Joseph considered this. "The machine knows when it is dark," he said at last. "That is most of the job."

He wrote the time in the ledger, and then, after a moment, he wrote something else beneath it, which he did not let her read.

Joseph's reply, 'The machine knows when it is dark. That is most of the job,' most likely reveals:
Aa quiet acceptance mixed with the sense that something the machine cannot do is being lost
Bhis belief that the machine will do the work better than he did
Canger at the department for replacing him
Dthat he wants to frighten his granddaughter about the boats
Show answer & solution

Correct answer: A. a quiet acceptance mixed with the sense that something the machine cannot do is being lost

Solution: He pauses ('considered this'), concedes that the machine covers 'most' of the job, and so implies a remainder (attention to the boats) it cannot cover. The tone is restrained, not angry, and he neither praises the machine as better nor tries to frighten the girl.
